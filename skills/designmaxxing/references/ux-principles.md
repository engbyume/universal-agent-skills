# UX principles, effects, and heuristics

Treat these as decision prompts, not a checklist or a set of universal laws. Some entries summarize findings from specific experiments. Others are design maxims or general heuristics. Use only the concepts that help answer a real design question. Check product evidence, accessibility, platform conventions, and representative user behavior before choosing a design.

The supplied list has 20 entries and 19 distinct principles because Postel's Law appears twice. The table keeps both numbered entries and marks the duplicate.

| # | Supplied term | Practical use and limits |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hick's Law, also called the Hick-Hyman Law | Decision time tends to increase with the information in a set of choices. Make meaningful choices easier to scan and compare. Do not hide needed options or assume fewer choices always improve the experience. [Hick, 1952](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470215208416600) |
| 2 | Fitts' Law | A pointing target is faster to acquire when it is larger or closer. Give frequent actions usable hit areas and place them near the relevant task when that fits the layout. Check the actual input method and platform target-size guidance. [Fitts, 1954](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0055392) |
| 3 | Jakob's Law | Users bring expectations from other products. Keep familiar patterns recognizable unless a change clearly helps the user or the task. Familiarity does not require copying every competitor. [Nielsen, 2000](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/end-of-web-design/) |
| 4 | Law of Proximity | Nearby elements tend to look related. Use spacing to show which labels, controls, and content belong together. Check that visual closeness does not imply a false relationship. [Wertheimer, 1923](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms.htm) |
| 5 | Miller's Law | Miller's 1956 paper covered specific limits in absolute judgment and memory tasks. Do not use 7 +/- 2 as a universal menu or screen limit. Group information only when the chunks help comprehension or recall. [Miller, 1956](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158); [Cowan, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922) |
| 6 | Doherty Threshold | Fast system feedback can help people stay engaged in interactive work. Measure frequent delays and acknowledge actions immediately. The often quoted 400 ms comes from a specific historical computing context, not a universal usability cutoff. [Doherty and Thadhani, 1982](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/system/files/inline-files/EconomicValueofResponseTime.pdf) |
| 7 | Von Restorff Effect | An item that stands apart from similar items may be easier to remember. Reserve strong emphasis for genuinely important choices or states. Too many highlighted items compete with one another. [von Restorff, 1933](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02409636) |
| 8 | Minimized Target Distance | Treat this as an application of Fitts' Law, not a separate established law. Reduce movement to frequent targets when the input method and layout support it. Do not shrink spacing or make nearby actions easy to confuse. [Fitts, 1954](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0055392) |
| 9 | Serial Position Effect | In list-recall tasks, people can recall items near the beginning or end better than items in the middle. Give important information a clear position and repeat it where needed. This does not prove that screen position alone determines task success. [Murdock, 1962](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045106) |
| 10 | Peak-End Rule | Retrospective evaluations can be influenced by an experience's most intense moment and its ending. Make important completion, error recovery, and handoff moments clear. Do not optimize only the ending. The classic evidence used aversive experiences, so do not assume every product journey follows the same pattern. [Kahneman et al., 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x) |
| 11 | Zeigarnik Effect | The original claim was that interrupted tasks can be remembered better than completed tasks. A 2025 meta-analysis found no general recall advantage, though it did find a tendency to resume unfinished tasks. Make saved progress and resume actions clear when they help. Do not create artificial open loops or pressure users to return. [Zeigarnik, 1927](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/willpower/1927-zeigarnik.pdf); [Seifert and Patalano, 1991](https://apatalano.faculty.wesleyan.edu/files/2019/07/Seifert_Patalano_1991.pdf); [2025 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) |
| 12 | Law of Prognosis | This label is unclear. It may mean the Gestalt Law of Prägnanz, which describes a tendency toward simple, stable perceptual organization, or it may refer to another Gestalt principle such as Good Continuation. Use the Prägnanz interpretation only when the context supports it. Simplify ambiguous visuals when it helps recognition, but preserve needed detail. Confirm the intended source if the distinction matters. [Wertheimer, 1923](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms.htm); [Gestalt review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4611091/); [Wagemans et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02344-9) |
| 13 | Law of Similarity | Elements that look alike tend to be perceived as related. Use consistent styling for elements with the same role. Do not use color alone to communicate a relationship or state. [Wertheimer, 1923](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms.htm) |
| 14 | Uniform Connected | The standard term is Uniform Connectedness. Items joined by a line or placed within a shared region tend to be perceived as a group. Use connections to clarify relationships or sequence, and ensure the underlying labels and structure agree. [Palmer and Rock, 1994](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03200760) |
| 15 | Tessler's Law | The standard spelling is Tesler's Law, also called the Law of Conservation of Complexity. Some task complexity may shift between the user and the system. Move avoidable work into the system when safe, while preserving user control and explaining consequential automation. [Tesler, original formulation](https://www.nomodes.com/larry-tesler-consulting/complexity-law) |
| 16 | Postel's Law | This comes from network protocol design. In UI work, accept harmless, unambiguous input variations when that helps users, but keep validation and security checks strict. Do not silently reinterpret ambiguous input or corrupt data. [RFC 793](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc793.html); [RFC 9413](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9413.html) |
| 17 | Postel's Law, repeated | This is the same principle as item 16, not a separate rule. Count it once and use the same limits. [RFC 9413](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9413.html) |
| 18 | Parkinson's Law | This began as a satirical observation that work can expand to fill the available time. Keep workflows focused, but do not impose artificial deadlines or urgency. [Parkinson, 1955](https://www.economist.com/news/1955/11/19/parkinsons-law) |
| 19 | Occam's Razor | Prefer the simplest design that adequately serves the task. Remove unnecessary steps, but preserve needed functionality, accessibility, error handling, and user control. This is a reasoning heuristic, not an empirical law of interface behavior. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simplicity/) |
| 20 | Pareto Principle | Use product evidence to find a small number of tasks or issues that may account for much of observed use or impact. Do not assume the ratio is exactly 80/20 or neglect uncommon, high-stakes, or accessibility paths. [American Economic Association review](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.6.2.181) |

When these principles conflict, prioritize the user's actual goal, accessible operation, product evidence, and the active design system. Prototype and test a relevant alternative instead of citing a principle as proof.
